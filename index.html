I work on how graph algorithms use GPUs, and on how much of a GPU they actually need. A graph traversal rarely keeps a whole data-center GPU busy, and how much of one it can use turns out to depend on the input graph rather than on the program. My thesis work builds a scheduler that reads the graph and picks the GPU partition from it.

The rest of my time goes to CUDA kernels. I rewrite published graph kernels with warp-level primitives and measure what the change is worth across large sets of real graphs. In the summer of 2026 I worked at Accenture on GPU inference infrastructure, which was a useful change of scale after a year spent inside warp scheduling.

Research

SliceFit: input-aware MIG slice selection

Submitted to IPDPS 2027. Under review.

NVIDIA's Multi-Instance GPU divides one GPU into isolated slices. Schedulers normally choose a job's slice from an earlier profiling run, which treats the right slice as a property of the program. For graph algorithms that assumption does not hold, because the input graph decides how much parallel work the GPU is given.

I measured four cuGraph algorithms (BFS, single-source shortest path, PageRank and betweenness centrality) on every MIG slice of two GPU generations, the A100 and the H100, over a corpus of 1,234 real-world graphs. BFS runs faster on a three-sevenths slice than on the largest one for 77 to 80% of graphs. Yet 88 to 97% of graphs with more than ten million edges still need the largest slice, so no fixed rule serves both.

SliceFit reads the graph file instead of profiling the job. It moves a job up one slice only while the speed gained outweighs the GPU capacity given up, against a threshold fitted per algorithm. A model trained once per algorithm predicts each step from ten degree features, read in 0.39 ms, so a new job never needs a profiling run. The median job stays as fast as it would be on the largest slice while 17 to 35% of the GPU is freed for other work.

Warp-level optimization of CUDA graph kernels

Graph kernels lose much of their time to atomic contention and to rescanning edges that have not changed. I reworked several published GPU kernels to cut both, and measured each change across large graph suites rather than on a handful of inputs.

BFS and SSSP from LonestarGPU, rewritten with warp-shuffle reductions and retuned launch parameters, improved on roughly 95% of the graphs I tested. For a Borŭvka minimum spanning tree kernel I packed edge weight and edge ID into a single sortable 64-bit key, which turns the best-edge choice into one deterministic atomicMin, cut the global atomics to one lane per warp with __shfl_down_sync, and replaced the per-round edge rescan with an edge-to-source map. Throughput improved on about 90% of 1,244 graphs.

Projects

Parallel algorithms across MPI, OpenMP, CUDA and HIP

Rewrote serial algorithms as MPI, pthreads, OpenMP, CUDA, multi-GPU and hybrid implementations, then measured them on GPU cluster nodes through Slurm. A persistent-thread minimum reduction reached about 24.6 times a single-threaded CPU baseline, and a GPU counting sort ran roughly 102 to 136 times faster than std::sort across the sizes tested.

Pop-CCL: popularity-adaptive long-tail recommendation

Extended the SimpleX collaborative filtering model with a popularity-adaptive cosine contrastive loss. Recall@20 for tail items rose by up to 56% on Yelp18, Amazon-Books and Gowalla, with little change to the implementation.

Federated learning under homomorphic encryption

Implemented federated learning with Paillier encryption on CIFAR-100 using the AIJack framework, so client updates stay encrypted through aggregation.

Parallel dead-block analysis for C compilers

Sped up dead-block detection with dynamic load balancing and parallel execution across GCC and LLVM builds at several optimization levels, using Csmith to generate the code and program markers to instrument it.
